RQ1 asked: *To what extent does SwinFusion++ improve video-level deepfake detection performance compared with standalone Swin Transformer and standalone Video Swin Transformer under in-distribution and cross-dataset evaluation, in terms of Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, and ROC-AUC?* The principal evidence is the fixed-recipe comparison, in which Swin-B, Video Swin-B, and SwinFusion++ were trained with the identical recipe, so a difference between them is not due to unequal tuning. The BO-selected SwinFusion++ is reported afterward as a configured system with unequal optimization effort.

All results come from held-out test videos that were not used for training, model selection, threshold selection, or calibration. Each model is the three-seed ensemble described in Chapter 3: a video’s score is the mean logit of its seven 16-frame clips, averaged over the three seeds, and the decision threshold and calibration temperature were fixed on source-validation videos before any test video was scored. Under cross-dataset transfer, the source-trained ensemble and its threshold were applied unchanged to the target dataset, and no target label was used for any decision. All results use the plain inference variant; results with horizontal-flip test-time augmentation are reported in Appendix J (Table J4).

Results are reported according to the analysis plan in Chapter 3. An interim decision to present the BO-selected configuration as primary was reverted to the approved plan; all comparisons under both regimens are reported. The statistical procedures, including the changes made after test scoring, are described in Chapter 3 under Statistical Treatment of Data, and the original and corrected permutation tests are compared in Appendix J (Table J5). The rank-based correction removed one significant difference favoring SwinFusion++ (within DeeperForensics-1.0, against Video Swin-B) and added two (Celeb-DF v2 → FF++ C23 and DeeperForensics-1.0 → Celeb-DF v2, both against Swin-B).

Table VI summarizes the four test sets, their dependence structure, and their coverage.

[Table VI. Test Sets, Dependence Structure, and Coverage  (source T1.csv)]

All test videos were analyzed. Coverage was 100%, and no video ended in a preprocessing failure or an insufficient-evidence state, so no result depends on how failed videos would have been counted. The test sets differ in their dependence structure. Each FaceForensics++ test set contains 70 source clusters of four videos, and DeeperForensics-1.0 contains 201 real–fake source pairs. Celeb-DF v2 contains 978 videos, but all 843 of its fake videos lie in nine target-identity clusters, so its fake class rests on nine independent clusters and its intervals are approximate. Because the clusters are keyed by target identity, whether the identity of the swapped-in source face was also held out could not be verified from the archived records.

Three features limit how cleanly the test sets are separated from model development. First, most FaceForensics++ and DeeperForensics-1.0 test videos share source lineage with another condition’s training or validation videos: 260 of 280 in each FaceForensics++ set and 382 of 402 in DeeperForensics-1.0. The Celeb-DF v2 test set has no recorded cross-condition overlap. Table VI gives these counts. Second, the preprocessing recovery rules were developed on 247 pooled training videos, 67 of which share lineage with held-out videos (15 physically identical). In addition, frames from 157 held-out videos were shown, without labels or scores, during face-quality review. No claim of untouched preprocessing selection is therefore made. Third, a metadata-only audit before training found that raw file metadata separated real from fake videos in Celeb-DF v2 (validation AUC 0.67) and DeeperForensics-1.0 (0.68). The models were then restricted to the rebuilt face-clip cache, and the repeated audit gave 0.50 on every dataset.

Two rules apply to every comparison below. First, ROC-AUC differences are paired: both models are evaluated on the same videos, and each bootstrap draw or permutation is applied to both. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 5,000 stratified cluster-bootstrap draws and are not adjusted for multiplicity, so the decision for each comparison is the Holm-adjusted p-value of the rank-based cluster permutation test; Holm-adjusted p-values below 0.005, which include the smallest attainable values, are written as < 0.005. Second, the inference is conditional on the trained ensembles: it describes how the evaluated models perform on new test videos, not how a retrained model would perform. Threshold-independent results (ROC-AUC and PR-AUC) and threshold-dependent results (accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score) are reported separately, and differences in threshold-dependent metrics are given only as supplementary paired intervals, without tests.

## Within-Dataset Performance

Table VII presents the within-dataset results of the three fixed-recipe models.

[Table VII. Within-Dataset Performance of the Fixed-Recipe Models  (source T4.csv)]

All three models reached ROC-AUC between 0.9878 and 0.9974 on every test set. SwinFusion++ had the highest point estimate of the three on DeeperForensics-1.0 (0.9974) and was within 0.0037 of the highest on the other three test sets (FF++ C23 0.9942, C40 0.9878, Celeb-DF v2 0.9951). In accuracy, it classified the most videos correctly of the three on DeeperForensics-1.0 (397 of 402, compared with 393 for Swin-B and 390 for Video Swin-B) and the second most on Celeb-DF v2 (956 of 978, compared with 933 and 961). On the FaceForensics++ test sets, the three models differed by at most three of 280 videos: SwinFusion++ matched Swin-B on C23 (97.86%) and C40 (94.64%), and Video Swin-B classified three more videos correctly on each. All three models therefore operated near the ceiling of the metric on every test set (ROC curves in Appendix J, Figure J1).

Table VIII presents the paired ROC-AUC differences between SwinFusion++ and each encoder.

[Table VIII. Paired ROC-AUC Differences Within Datasets, Fixed Recipe  (source T5.csv)]

No ROC-AUC difference between SwinFusion++ and either encoder was detectable (all Holm-adjusted p = 1.000; raw p from 0.198 to 0.925), and every paired 95% interval lay between −0.011 and +0.009 AUC, which is incompatible with a large difference in either direction. SwinFusion++ had the lower point estimate in six of the eight comparisons. The ceiling left little room to detect a gain: in seven of the eight comparisons, even the largest gain still possible was smaller than the difference the test could detect with 80% power (Appendix J, Table J6), so these results do not establish equivalence. In the supplementary paired intervals for threshold-dependent metrics (Table J2), SwinFusion++ had higher accuracy than Swin-B on Celeb-DF v2 (+2.35 percentage points [+0.08, +5.10]; approximate, nine fake-class clusters) and than Video Swin-B on DeeperForensics-1.0 (+1.74 percentage points [+0.50, +3.23]). Because the corresponding ROC-AUC differences were not detectable, these gains are best read as differences in where the frozen thresholds fell rather than as better separation of real and fake videos. Within each benchmark, SwinFusion++ was therefore statistically indistinguishable from both encoders in ROC-AUC: combining the two encoders did not lower in-distribution ROC-AUC by a detectable amount, every paired interval excludes a loss larger than 0.011, and all three models operated near the ceiling of the metric.

Under frozen transfer, SwinFusion++ had a significantly higher ROC-AUC than Swin-B in seven of the nine directions, by +0.049 to +0.258 (Holm-adjusted p from < 0.005 to 0.018), and than Video Swin-B in three, Celeb-DF v2 → FF++ C40 (+0.0754), Celeb-DF v2 → DeeperForensics-1.0 (+0.0630), and DeeperForensics-1.0 → FF++ C40 (+0.0840), all with Holm-adjusted p < 0.005. SwinFusion++ was significantly lower than an encoder in none of the 18 comparisons. This is not evidence of non-inferiority: the five negative point estimates were not significant, but the widest interval reached −0.092 (DeeperForensics-1.0 → Celeb-DF v2, against Video Swin-B). SwinFusion++ also had the highest point estimate in FF++ C23 → C40 and DeeperForensics-1.0 → FF++ C23 (heatmap), but its lead over the runner-up, Video Swin-B, was not significant in either direction (+0.0014; and +0.0451 [+0.0077, +0.0816], Holm-adjusted p = 0.152, shown with an open marker in the forest plot).

From FF++ C23, SwinFusion++ was statistically indistinguishable from both encoders on FF++ C40 and Celeb-DF v2, and the one significant difference was the largest of all 18: on DeeperForensics-1.0, Swin-B’s ROC-AUC fell to 0.5678 [0.5250, 0.6105], while the two models with a temporal stream kept 0.8261 (SwinFusion++, +0.258 over Swin-B) and 0.8421 (Video Swin-B). This pattern is consistent with temporal evidence transferring better than frame appearance in this direction. Swin-B and Video Swin-B also differ in pretraining and input handling, however, so the comparison does not isolate the cause.

The significant differences were stable across seeds and analysis choices. Nine of the ten had the same sign in all nine pairings of individual seeds, and the tenth (Celeb-DF v2 → FF++ C40 against Video Swin-B) in eight of nine; all ten remained significant under the family grouping recorded before scoring and under one pooled family, and no DeeperForensics-1.0-target decision changed with 175 lineage clusters (Appendix J, Table J7). Two limits apply. Transfer lowered ROC-AUC for every model, to between 0.459 and 0.976, and from DeeperForensics-1.0 to Celeb-DF v2 all three models were near chance (0.4593 to 0.6001). The three significant differences from DeeperForensics-1.0 to FF++ C23 and C40 could not be checked meaningfully on a lineage-free subset, because only 20 such test videos exist (Table VI; per-seed values in Table J3). Under matched training, SwinFusion++ therefore ranked target videos significantly better than Swin-B in seven of the nine transfer directions and than Video Swin-B in three. Whether these differences can be attributed to TLBG-HCA, rather than to the combination of two encoders, is examined under RQ4 with the topology and capacity-matched controls.

RQ1 also concerns accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Table X gives these metrics at the frozen source thresholds, and the ROC curves in the figure after it show where each frozen threshold fell on the target curve.

At the frozen source thresholds, every model became conservative on the target data. Specificity stayed between 91.11% and 100.00%, while recall was below 64% in every direction except FF++ C23 → C40 (79.29% to 87.14%). From the Celeb-DF v2 and DeeperForensics-1.0 sources, no model flagged more than 60 test videos as fake, so accuracy in those directions mainly reflects the share of real videos. From DeeperForensics-1.0 to Celeb-DF v2, where 86.20% of the test videos are fake, accuracy was only 13.80% to 15.24%, close to the 13.80% obtained by calling every video real.

SwinFusion++ did not have the highest accuracy, recall, or F1-score in any of the nine directions (point estimates; several margins were one or two videos). From FF++ C23, its high precision and specificity reflect that it made the fewest positive calls of the three models (114, 458, and 8 on C40, Celeb-DF v2, and DeeperForensics-1.0); from the other two sources, Swin-B made the fewest or as few as any other model. From FF++ C23 to Celeb-DF v2, for example, SwinFusion++ raised only six false alarms among 135 real videos (specificity 95.56%) but detected 53.62% of the fake videos, and its accuracy (59.41%) was lower than that of Swin-B (67.48%) and Video Swin-B (66.46%), although the three ROC-AUC values did not differ detectably. In the supplementary paired intervals (Table J2), its transfer accuracy was lower than an encoder’s in 6 of 18 comparisons (both encoders from FF++ C23 to C40 and to Celeb-DF v2; Video Swin-B from FF++ C23 to DeeperForensics-1.0 and from Celeb-DF v2 to C23) and higher in 2 (Swin-B from Celeb-DF v2 to C23 and to DeeperForensics-1.0).

The ROC curves illustrate the split between ROC-AUC and accuracy. Outside FF++ C23 → C40, where ROC-AUC stayed near or above 0.8, the curves show that the ranking of fake above real videos was largely retained, but the frozen operating point sat in the low-recall part of the curve. Moving it would require target labels, which the frozen-transfer design excludes. Where ROC-AUC was near 0.5, as from DeeperForensics-1.0 to Celeb-DF v2, the ranking itself was close to chance, so the problem was not only the threshold. For all three models, the frozen screening label on an unseen dataset is therefore much less informative than the ranking results suggest.

## Discussion of RQ1

In distribution, under the principal fixed-recipe comparison, SwinFusion++ performed at the same near-ceiling level as standalone Swin-B and Video Swin-B. All three models reached ROC-AUC of 0.988 to 0.997, no paired ROC-AUC difference was detectable, and each paired interval lay between −0.011 and +0.009 AUC; at this ceiling, little room remained for a detectable improvement. The threshold-dependent metrics were similarly close: accuracy differed by at most three videos on FaceForensics++, and precision and recall differed from those of either encoder by at most 2.0 and 3.1 percentage points. The only supplementary accuracy and F1-score intervals that excluded zero favored SwinFusion++ (Celeb-DF v2 against Swin-B; DeeperForensics-1.0 against Video Swin-B); because the ROC-AUC differences there were not detectable, these gains are best read as threshold placement rather than better ranking. This near-ceiling pattern agrees with the benchmark literature, in which strong detectors reach very high accuracy when training and test videos come from the same benchmark (Rössler et al., 2019), whereas detectors trained on one dataset perform much worse on a dataset they have not seen (Li et al., 2020).

Under frozen cross-dataset evaluation, SwinFusion++ had significantly higher ROC-AUC than Swin-B in seven of the nine directions (by +0.049 to +0.258) and than Video Swin-B in three (by +0.063 to +0.084), and in those three it was significantly better than both encoders. It was not significantly lower than either encoder in any direction, although the widest interval reached −0.092, and nine of the ten significant differences had the same sign in every pairing of individual seeds. The advantage came mainly from the Celeb-DF v2 and DeeperForensics-1.0 sources; from FF++ C23, the one detectable difference was also the largest, +0.258 against Swin-B on DeeperForensics-1.0. This is the setting that motivated SwinFusion++, which combines frame appearance with temporal evidence: detectors that exploit temporal information, such as mouth movements or temporal coherence, have been reported to perform better on unseen manipulations and datasets than frame-based detectors (Haliassos et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2021). The present results are consistent with that account, while the attribution of the gain to TLBG-HCA itself is tested under RQ4.

At the frozen source thresholds, however, SwinFusion++ did not have the highest transfer accuracy, recall, or F1-score in any direction (point estimates; several margins were one or two videos), and in the supplementary paired intervals its transfer accuracy was lower than an encoder’s in 6 of 18 comparisons and higher in 2 (Table J2). This limitation is shared by all three models and follows from the frozen-transfer design: with thresholds set on each training source’s score distribution, every model flagged fewer target videos as fake than the target set contained, which kept specificity at 91% or higher but lowered recall. ROC-AUC measures the ranking of fake above real videos without depending on that threshold, which is why ROC-AUC is the primary measure in this study (Chapter 3); in practice, a detector used on a new kind of video needs its operating point re-established with labeled examples from that setting.

Relative to the fixed recipe, the BO-selected configuration significantly raised ROC-AUC within FF++ C40 and Celeb-DF v2 (the latter depending on the family grouping) and from FF++ C23 to C40, but significantly lowered it in five other transfer directions, consistent with tighter fitting to each training source. Tuning for source-validation ROC-AUC therefore did not translate into better transfer, in line with the broader finding that gains measured on the training distribution need not carry over to new data (Geirhos et al., 2020). Across the nine transfer directions, the fixed recipe ranked target videos significantly better than the BO-selected configuration in five, worse in one, and no detectably differently in three. These results support the use of the fixed-recipe configuration for the web service’s detectors, because videos submitted to the service generally come from outside the detector’s training benchmark (even the built-in sample clips were never offered to a detector trained on the same dataset; Chapter 3), a setting closer to cross-dataset transfer than to within-dataset testing.

The improvement that RQ1 asks about is therefore present in ranking performance under frozen transfer, in seven of nine directions against Swin-B and in three of nine against Video Swin-B, with no direction in which SwinFusion++ was significantly worse than either encoder. Within datasets, where all three models were near the ceiling, no improvement was detectable, and under transfer the ranking advantage did not carry over to threshold-dependent performance at the frozen source operating points. Whether the transfer differences are due to TLBG-HCA is examined under RQ4, and the verdict against the Chapter 3 criterion for a strong model is given under RQ6.
